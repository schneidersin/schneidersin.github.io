Hamster Roll 3D Privacy Policy

Last updated: 15 November 2026
This policy is for the game Hamster Roll 3D for Android phones and tablets, from Google Play.

For kids: the short version

  • You can play without telling the game your name, your email or where you live. The game asks what grade you are in at school, so the quiz questions are right for you (and, before a few choices, the year you were born).
  • Your races, medals and seeds are kept on your phone or tablet.
  • The game also keeps a list of your races and the quiz questions you got wrong, on your phone or tablet. A grown-up can look at it in Settings, to help you learn.
  • Want a copy of your game kept safe on the internet, in case your phone gets lost? Tap PLAY AS A GUEST (BACKED UP). Your races, seeds and hamster name are then kept at Google too. You don't need an email for that.
  • You can also tap Sign in with Google. The game first asks the year you were born. It doesn't save that year or send it anywhere. Signing in with Google gives the game the email address, name and picture of that Google account.
  • The game shows ads from Google. The game asks Google to show only ads that are OK for kids, and not to choose them from things about you. If an ad looks strange or asks you to do something, don't tap it. Ask a grown-up.
  • In online games, other players see your hamster's name, colour and outfit. You can only send quick messages like GG!, so you can't type to anyone.
  • Games you host are private: only friends with your room code can join. Making a game public, or changing chat, needs a grown-up.
  • If someone bothers you in a game, tap the speaker button next to their name so you don't see their messages, and tell a grown-up.
  • Never tell anyone in a game your real name, your school, where you live or your phone number.
  • You or a grown-up can delete your backup at any time on the ACCOUNT screen.
  • If something here is hard to understand, ask a parent or another grown-up to read it with you.

The rest of this page is written for parents and other grown-ups. It explains exactly what the game does with information, in the order you meet it in the game.

3. Backing up the game (optional)

A backup keeps the player's progress safe if the game is reinstalled. It is off until someone taps SIGN IN on the main menu and then PLAY AS A GUEST (BACKED UP) or Sign in with Google on the ACCOUNT screen. (If Android's own backup puts the game back on a device, a backup that was on before is on again there.) Without it the game works fully, and nothing in this section happens.

The backup is a guest account: it has no name and no email address, only a random account ID, which the ACCOUNT screen shows as the "Account code". Players who are surely 13 or older can use their Google account instead (see "Signing in with Google" below). The phone and tablet game has no other kind of sign-in (no email address and password). Backups are stored with Google Firebase, a service I use to keep them. Google stores them for me.

What a backup holds

  • the progress listed in section 2, except the school grade: seeds, outfits, the number of races finished and whether Remove ads was bought are included;
  • the settings that follow a player to another device: the hamster's name and colour, sound and music levels, screen shake, camera, hints, vibration, laps and computer racers;
  • the install labels of the devices that have used the backup, the time of the last upload, and the date the backup will be deleted if it isn't used (section 10).

The game uploads a few seconds after progress or settings change, and downloads the backup when the game starts and after signing in. While the game is open it also renews the sign-in with Google about once an hour.

The database rules let only that account read or change its own backup, and refuse every other player. As the owner of the database, I can also reach backups through Firebase's own tools, and Google runs the database for me.

When the game talks to Firebase, Google also receives the device's IP address and basic technical details of the request, such as the phone's model and Android version, as for any internet connection. Google says Firebase Authentication uses the IP address and these details for security and to prevent abuse, and keeps logged IP addresses for a few weeks. For each account, Firebase keeps the account ID and the dates the account was made and last used. For an account signed in with Google it keeps more (below).

Signing in with Google (players 13 and older)

On the ACCOUNT screen there is also Sign in with Google (for a guest, Continue with Google). Before Google's sign-in opens, the game asks a neutral question, under the name of the button that was tapped: "Type the year you were born". It doesn't say which years are accepted, and it says nothing about ages or grown-ups. Google's sign-in opens only if the year is at least 14 years before this one (by the device's clock when the year is typed), so the player is surely 13 or older. Anyone younger is told kindly that they can keep playing without Google, and Google's sign-in doesn't open: they can still use the guest backup. A year that can't be right (in the future, or more than 120 years ago) is not kept, and the question is asked again.

The game asks the same neutral question, under their own names, before a hosted game is made public and before Chat is changed (section 4), and one answer serves all of them. So a year typed earlier while the game is open, for example by a parent making a game public, also answers the question before Google's sign-in. The game keeps only how many years ago the year was, worked out once when it is typed, and only in its memory while it runs. It is never saved on the device or sent anywhere. Once a year has been typed, the game doesn't take another until it is closed and started again, so a child can't simply try another year. The one exception: once the game has been out of sight for 5 minutes, an answer that let something through is asked again before it lets anything through again, in case someone else is now holding the device. The new answer can only make the player younger, never older. The question can't prove anyone's age.

If the player goes on, Google's own sign-in screen on the phone lets them pick a Google account. Google says that Sign in with Google shares the name, email address and profile picture of that Google account with the app (Google's description). Google gives the game a sign-in token holding these, and the game passes it straight to Firebase Authentication, which signs the player in. For that account, Firebase then keeps the Google account's email address, its name, the web address of its profile picture and Google's own ID for the Google account, besides the account ID and dates above. On the device, the game keeps only the email address (section 2) and shows it on the ACCOUNT screen. The email address, name and picture are not part of the backup itself, and the game never shows them to other players. The backup holds the same things as a guest's (listed above), and the database rules protect it in the same way.

A guest who taps Continue with Google keeps the same Account code and backup: the guest account becomes the Google account. If that Google account already has an account of its own in the game, the game signs in to that one instead and adds this device's progress to its backup. It then deletes the guest's backup and the guest account, as everything in them is now in the Google account's backup. If it can't (for example, the device was offline when the game started, so the guest backup wasn't read, or the deletion fails), the guest backup and account are left as they are. The game no longer shows their Account code, and they are deleted on the timetable in section 10.

Google's sign-in screen is run by Google under Google's Privacy Policy. Google also lists the game among the apps that the player has signed in to with their Google account. The player can see and remove that link in their Google Account at myaccount.google.com/linkedapps. Removing it doesn't delete the game's account or backup (section 11 explains how).

If an email account was made with an earlier test version of the game, it keeps working on that device: it can still be saved, signed out and deleted on the ACCOUNT screen, and section 11 explains how to delete it without the game.

4. Playing online and on Wi-Fi

The game sends nothing to other devices until a player opens HOST ONLINE or JOIN ONLINE. Online games are run on the players' own devices: one player HOST ONLINEs and the others JOIN ONLINE. There is no game server of mine, so I never receive anything from online games. A phone can also join a game hosted on a computer by typing its address. Whoever runs that computer receives what any host receives, and the separate server program for computers keeps a record of each race (every player's steering, school grade and times, without names) unless it is told not to.

Both screens show a reminder: "Stay safe: never tell anyone in a game your real name, school, address or phone number."

What other players in the same game receive

  • the hamster's name (on phones, "Phone" or one of the 10 names from the list), colour, hat and fur;
  • the player's school grade number, so each player gets quiz questions for their own grade (the game doesn't show it as a number, though someone watching that player sees the questions for that grade);
  • the player's steering and button presses, and the race as it happens: positions, falls, places and times;
  • quick chat: phones can send only 6 fixed phrases (GG!, NICE ONE!, SORRY!, OOPS!, WAIT FOR ME!, HELP ME!) and 4 pictures (heart, laugh, angry, star).

No typed messages on phones. The phone and tablet game can't send typed messages. If a computer player types one, the phone game doesn't show it. If a phone hosts a game that computer players join, they can still type lines to each other through it; the phone never shows or keeps them. Players on computers can type their own hamster names, though, and phones show those names (and the names of games hosted on computers), with a list of common English swear words starred out. A filter can't catch every unkind name. A player can leave such a game, and the host can remove a player.

Muting. Players can mute another player with the speaker button next to their name (or PLAYERS: MUTE in the pause menu). Muting hides that player's quick chat for the rest of that game only; their name and hamster still show. There is no way to report a player inside the game: parents can email me (section 17).

Grown-up controls. A hosted game is private by default: it needs its 5-digit room code to join. On phones and tablets, making a game public and changing Settings > Chat (quick chat on or off) both need the grown-up check first: a neutral screen, titled with what was tapped, asks for the year the person was born, and the change is allowed only if that year is at least 18 years before this one. It is the same question that comes before Google sign-in (section 3), and one answer serves all of them: the game keeps only how many years ago the year was, in its memory, never saved or sent anywhere, and it answers every later check without asking again. If the year is less than 18 years before this one, these choices stay closed until the game is closed and started again. (A year at least 14 years before this one still lets that player sign in with Google.) After a grown-up passes the check, these choices, and Google sign-in, stay open to whoever holds the device while the game stays on screen. Once the game has been out of sight for 5 minutes, the question is asked again before anything opens, and the new answer can only make the player younger, never older: a younger player's answer closes what the grown-up's opened. The check can't prove anyone's age, so please keep these choices for grown-ups.

Network addresses. To connect, devices must know each other's IP address. While the JOIN ONLINE screen is open, the device asks once a second for games on the same Wi-Fi and at any saved addresses, so devices hosting there see its address even before it joins. The host's device receives the IP address of everyone who joins; the game doesn't show these on screen and keeps them only while it hosts (for example, to keep out a player the host removed). Anyone on the same Wi-Fi who opens JOIN ONLINE sees a public game's host address next to its name, and players in a game see the host's address at the bottom of the screen. Players who join don't receive each other's addresses.

Who can see a hosted game. While a player hosts a public game, anyone on the same Wi-Fi who opens JOIN ONLINE sees it as "name's game", with the host's address, the race and how many players are in it. Someone elsewhere who has saved the host's address can see it too, if their device can reach the host's. A private game stays off that list until someone on the same Wi-Fi types any 5-digit code and taps JOIN WITH CODE. Then they see its name, the host's address and how many players are in it, but they still need the right code to get in. Everyone in a private game can see its room code, so a friend can pass it on. On a shared or public Wi-Fi network, the people who see a game may be strangers.

No encryption between players. Online game messages go straight between each player's device and the host's device and are not encrypted, so someone with the right tools on the same Wi-Fi, or on the internet path between them when friends join from elsewhere, could read them. They hold only the game information listed above, the game's name and room code, and any lines typed by computer players, which a phone receives but never shows.

After an online game ends, nothing about the other players (their names, messages or addresses) is kept. The device keeps only the saved game address mentioned in section 2 and the player's own progress from the game, such as seeds earned, stars found and the number of races finished. Players can choose not to play online at all.

5. Ads

The game is free and shows ads from Google AdMob, which is self-certified for apps for children under Google Play's Families rules.

When ads appear

  • An ad can come up as a player leaves a race's results, but never after any of a player's first 5 races, at most once every 3 races after that, never in online or Wi-Fi games, never between the races of a cup, and never after the training course.
  • From the first race, the results of a race played on this device can offer WATCH AN AD: DOUBLE SEEDS. An ad plays only if the player taps it.

How the ads are limited

Every ad request from the game tells Google to:

  • treat the player as a child;
  • show only ads rated G (general audiences);
  • turn personalised ads off, so ads are not chosen from a profile of the player.

The game also removes the permission for Google Play services' advertising ID, and Android's ad topics and ad measurement permissions, so the ads can't use them.

What Google's ads software collects

Google's ads software in the game starts each time the game opens. It talks to Google even when no ad is on screen, because it gets an ad ready in advance. According to Google's own description, it collects:

  • the device's IP address, which can show the general area (for example the country or city);
  • how the app is used: app launches, taps and video views;
  • diagnostics: how quickly the app starts, hangs and energy use;
  • device and account identifiers: the app set ID, which is shared by apps from the same developer on the device, and, where they apply, other identifiers linked to accounts signed in on the device. The advertising ID is not one of them, because the game removes the permission for it.

Google says its ads software collects and shares this information for advertising (showing ads, limiting how often one repeats, and measuring them), for analytics and to prevent fraud, and that it is encrypted on the way. Google may also keep cookies or similar data on the device for these purposes, as its page How Google uses cookies explains. Google describes how it uses information from apps that use its ads services on its page How Google uses information from sites or apps that use our services, and in Google's Privacy Policy.

Each time it starts, the game asks Google's consent service whether a consent message is needed. Because the game tells Google that its players may be under the age of consent, Google's consent service doesn't ask them for consent, and the ads are limited in the ways listed above instead. If Google ever says a consent choice is needed, the game shows Google's consent message, and a PRIVACY OPTIONS button appears in Settings > ADS to change it.

6. Buying Remove ads

In Settings > ADS, Remove ads can be bought once with real money through Google Play. The game doesn't ask its own grown-up question first. Google Play's rules say it asks for verification (such as the Google account's password) before every purchase in a game designed for ages 12 and under, so a grown-up has to approve it.

  • Google Play handles the whole payment. The game and I never see card or bank details.
  • Google Play shows me details of each order in my developer account and its reports: the order number, the date and time, the product, the price and tax, the model of the device used, and the buyer's country, state or region and postcode (and sometimes town or city). If someone gives me the buyer's email address, I can look up that order.
  • Each time the game starts, comes back on screen or opens Settings, it asks the Google Play app on the device whether this Google account owns Remove ads, and what it costs here, so a purchase made on another device or before a reinstall comes back by itself. RESTORE PURCHASES asks again. The game keeps only whether Remove ads is owned, on the device and in the backup if one is on.
  • Google Play's billing software in the game may also send Google its own technical logs, under Google's Privacy Policy.

Google's Google Payments Privacy Notice covers the payment.

Remove ads stops the ads between races. The optional WATCH AN AD: DOUBLE SEEDS stays, so Google's ads software still starts and collects the information in section 5.

7. What the game never does

  • It never asks Android for the device's location, camera, microphone, contacts, photos or other files, and it has no permission to use any of them. It reads and writes only its own files in its private storage. Google's ads software can still estimate the general area from the IP address, as section 5 says.
  • It never uses the advertising ID.
  • It contains no analytics or crash-reporting software of its own. Android and Google Play may send crash and performance reports to Google if the device owner has allowed sharing usage and diagnostics data in Android's settings. I can then see these reports in my developer account, for example the device model, the Android version and where in the game the problem happened.
  • It never asks Google for personalised ads, and I never build a profile of a player.
  • I never sell information, and never give it to anyone except as described on this page.
  • It never asks a child for their name, email address, home address, school or phone number. Google sign-in, which gives the game a Google account's email address and name, is offered only after a neutral year-of-birth question gives a year that makes the player surely 13 or older (section 3).

9. Children's privacy and parents' rights

Hamster Roll 3D is made for children and older players (its quizzes cover school grades 1 to 10, about ages 6 to 16), and many of its players are children. Under the US Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) and similar laws elsewhere, I follow these rules:

  • No contact details are asked from children. Children can play, back up and play online without giving a name, email address or any other contact details. The game asks for a school grade (1 to 10), to choose quiz questions, and keeps it on the device and in an online game's quiz signs (sections 2 and 4). Before a few choices it also asks the year of birth (sections 3 and 4): the game keeps only how many years ago that year was, in its memory while it runs, and never saves or sends it anywhere.
  • Google sign-in only for players 13 and older. Signing in with Google gives the game a Google account's email address and name, so on phones and tablets it comes after a neutral year-of-birth question (the same one that comes before a public game or a change to chat, section 4). The question doesn't say which years are accepted, and Google's sign-in opens only for a year at least 14 years before this one. Younger players are not let through (though the question can't prove anyone's age): their backup is a guest account, with no name or email. If a parent finds that a child under 13 has signed in with Google anyway, the parent can delete that account in the game or ask me to delete it (section 11).
  • Some technical identifiers are used without asking. These are the device's IP address and the app set ID (used by Google's ads software), and, only if a backup is on, the guest account ID and the install labels. They are used only for these internal operations: making the game, the backup and online play work; showing ads that are not personalised and limiting how often they repeat; measuring ads; and security and fraud prevention. How I make sure they are used for nothing else: the game has no analytics of its own and doesn't send them to me; every ad request tells Google to treat the player as a child with personalised ads turned off, and the advertising ID permission is removed from the game; the database lets only the account itself read its backup; and I never link these identifiers to a name or contact details or use them to contact anyone. Google says that with these settings it turns off personalised ads and remarketing.
  • Other players receive only game information: the hamster's name ("Phone" or one picked from a list), its look, the school grade number (used to pick quiz questions, not shown), quick chat and the race, plus the network addresses needed to connect (section 4). On phones children can't type messages or choose their own name, so they can't make their contact details public in the game.
  • Grown-ups control the online features. Before a child hosts or joins an online game, the game reminds them not to share personal details. Games are private by default, and only a grown-up who passes the grown-up check can make a game public or change the chat setting (section 4).

Parents can:

  • see what the game holds in the game's own screens: progress, settings, Settings > PLAYTEST LOG, the saved games on the JOIN ONLINE screen and the ACCOUNT screen. A backup holds the same progress and settings as the device (without the school grade).
  • correct it: change the hamster's name, colour and school grade in Settings, or email me.
  • have it deleted: see section 11.
  • stop further collection:
    • don't turn on a backup, or delete it;
    • switch chat off in Settings, or don't play online;
    • keep the device offline while playing (for example in flight mode). Nothing reaches Google while the device is offline and no ads appear, but Google's ads software may send its usual information the next time the game is opened with a connection;
    • or uninstall the game.

Write to me at blackaurorastar@gmail.com with any question, and I will answer within 30 days.

11. How to delete information

In the game

  • Backup and account: open ACCOUNT, tap DELETE ACCOUNT, then within 4 seconds tap TAP AGAIN TO DELETE FOR GOOD. This needs an internet connection. It deletes the backup at Google Firebase first, then the account itself, and the game signs out. Progress on the device stays, so the player can keep playing. If something goes wrong, the game says what was and wasn't deleted. A guest stays signed in and can tap DELETE ACCOUNT again; until a guest account is deleted, the game keeps backing it up. For an account signed in with Google, deleting the account also deletes the email address, name and other details Firebase kept from the Google account. Google deletes such an account only if it signed in recently. If it didn't, or if anything else stops the account being deleted, DELETE ACCOUNT still deletes the backup, signs out and says so. To delete the account too, tap Sign in with Google again, then straight away tap DELETE ACCOUNT and TAP AGAIN TO DELETE FOR GOOD. An email account from an earlier test version can't sign in again on a phone: if DELETE ACCOUNT can't delete it, email me from its address (below).
  • Signing out is not deleting. SIGN OUT only forgets the account on this device. 12. Keeping information safe

  • The game's connections to Google Firebase use encrypted connections (HTTPS), and Google says its ads software encrypts what it sends.
  • The database rules let each account read and change only its own backup, and they refuse everyone else.
  • The sign-in key (and a Google account's email address) is kept in the game's private storage on the device. If Android's own backup is on, it is also in that backup, which Android encrypts with the screen lock on Android 9 and later.
  • Online game messages between players are not encrypted (section 4).

No system is perfectly safe. If something ever went wrong with information I am responsible for, I would put a notice on this page, tell the people affected where I have a way to reach them, and tell the authorities, as the law requires.
